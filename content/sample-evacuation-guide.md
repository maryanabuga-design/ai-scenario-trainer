# Building Evacuation and Basic First Aid: Training Guide

Educational material for the prototype. It is an original, simplified summary and does not replace certified first-aid or fire-safety training. Local procedures take priority.

## S1. Responding to an alarm
- Treat every alarm as real until it is officially confirmed otherwise.
- Stop non-essential tasks. Take only what you can carry in a few seconds; never delay your exit to collect belongings.
- Do not use elevators during a fire. Use the stairs.
- Help people who need assistance only if it is safe. Otherwise report their location to responders.

### Why every alarm is treated as real

- Nobody can tell from the sound alone whether an alarm is a drill, a fault or a real fire, so the only safe assumption is that it is real.
- In many buildings alarms are caused by tests and faults. This is exactly why people stop reacting to them, and a delayed reaction to a real alarm costs the time the exit needs.
- An alarm is treated as real until building management, a warden or the emergency services officially say that it is not. A message from a colleague, a rumour or the alarm falling silent is not official confirmation.
- Take drills seriously. The way you act in a drill is the way you are likely to act in a real alarm.

### The first seconds

- Stop what you are doing. Do not finish the sentence, the email, the call or the exercise.
- Do not spend time saving files, shutting down equipment or tidying up. Anything that can be left as it is should be left.
- Alert the people around you by voice, clearly and briefly: "Alarm, leave now." Do not assume that everyone has heard it.
- Start moving towards the exit at once. If nobody else reacts, be the person who starts.

### What to take and what to leave

- Take only what is already in your hand or can be picked up in a few seconds, for example a phone, glasses or medicine you need and can reach.
- Leave coats, bags, laptops, keys on desks, files and anything heavy or bulky. Belongings can be replaced, time cannot.
- Never go back for an item, even if it is only a few metres away. A short detour delays you, blocks other people and may take you towards the danger.
- Keep one hand free so that you can hold the handrail on the stairs.

### Stairs, not elevators

- Do not use ordinary elevators during a fire alarm. A car can stop between floors, open onto a floor affected by the fire or stop when the power fails.
- Use the stairs even if an elevator is waiting at your floor and would be faster.
- Only lifts designated for evacuation and operated by the emergency services or trained staff may be used in an evacuation.
- On the stairs, walk at a steady pace and hold the handrail. Do not run, push or stop on the landings, because a fall or a stop blocks everyone behind you.
- Keep to one side so that responders who may be coming up can pass.

### Helping other people

- Help only if it is safe for you. A rescuer who becomes a casualty is a second problem for the responders.
- Safe help includes telling people to leave, guiding people who are confused and offering a steady arm to someone who walks slowly on a clear route.
- Do not try to carry or drag a person down the stairs unless you are trained and it is safe. It can injure both of you and block the stairs.
- If someone cannot leave without help, tell them that you will report where they are, and do so immediately to a warden or responder. Give the floor, the room, the nearest landing and the number of people with them.
- Do not go back into the building to look for someone. Report their name and last known location instead.

### Instructions and announcements

- Follow the instructions of wardens, announcements and the emergency services before your own guess.
- If an announcement or a warden directs you to a particular exit, use that exit.
- Do not take it as a sign of safety that other people are staying where they are. Often nobody moves because everybody is waiting for somebody else.
- Do not rely on messages in a chat or on the phone to decide whether an alarm is real. Only an official confirmation counts.

### Common mistakes

- Waiting to see what other people do.
- Finishing a task, a call or a document first.
- Going back for belongings.
- Using an ordinary elevator.
- Assuming it is a drill because the last alarm was one.
- Going back inside because the alarm has stopped.
- Entering the building to look for someone instead of reporting their location.

## S2. Checking the route
- Before opening a closed door, feel it with the back of your hand. If it is hot, do not open it. Use another route.
- If the door is cool, open it slowly and be ready to close it at once if you see smoke or feel heat.
- Close doors behind you. This slows the spread of fire and smoke.

## S3. Moving through smoke
- Smoke and hot gases rise. Stay low, where the air is cleaner, and cover your nose and mouth with cloth if possible.
- Keep one hand on the wall to hold your direction and move to the nearest safe exit.
- Do not break windows to clear smoke from a corridor. Extra air can make a fire grow.
- If your route is blocked, go to a room with a window, close the door, seal gaps if you can, signal your position and call the emergency number.

## S4. Assembly point and headcount
- Go to the designated assembly point, away from the building and its entrances so responders have access.
- Report to the person responsible for the headcount. Report anyone missing and their last known location.
- Do not re-enter the building until it is declared safe by responders.

## S5. Calling for help
- Call 112 or the local emergency number. Give the location, what happened, the number of injured people and any hazards. Stay on the line until told to hang up.
- Assign one specific person to call, for example: "You, call 112 and tell me what they said." A general request leads people to assume someone else has already called.

## S6. Assessing an injured person
- Check that the scene is safe first. Do not become a second casualty.
- Check responsiveness (speak to the person and gently tap the shoulders), then breathing (look, listen and feel for up to 10 seconds).
- Deal with life-threatening problems first: not breathing and severe bleeding.

## S7. Severe bleeding
- Press firmly and directly on the wound with a clean cloth or dressing, and keep the pressure on.
- If the cloth soaks through, add more layers on top instead of removing it.
- If a limb wound bleeds heavily and pressure does not control it, a tourniquet may be applied by someone trained and equipped. Note the time.
- Keep the person lying down and warm, and monitor them until help arrives.

## S8. Burns
- Cool the burn under cool running water for 10 to 20 minutes.
- Do not apply ice, butter, oils or toothpaste. Do not burst blisters.
- Cover loosely with a clean, non-fluffy dressing.
- Seek medical help for large or deep burns, burns on the face, hands or genitals, or if you are unsure.

## S9. Unresponsive person
- If the person is breathing normally, place them in the recovery position, keep the airway open and monitor breathing.
- If the person is not breathing normally, call the emergency number and start chest compressions in the centre of the chest, hard and fast (about 100 to 120 per minute). Use an AED if one is available and follow its prompts.

## S10. Handover to responders
- Give a short, ordered handover: what happened, what you found, what you did and when, and any changes since.
- Name any missing person and the last place they were seen.
