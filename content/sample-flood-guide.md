# Flood Preparedness for Community Volunteers: Training Guide

Educational material for volunteers and junior responders in civil protection. It covers preparation, response and recovery. It contains no combat, weapons or harm content.

## F1. Know your risk and your warnings

Find out whether your building, street or route is near a river, a low-lying area or a drainage channel. Learn how official warnings reach you: sirens, mobile alerts, local radio and the municipal website.

A flood **warning** means flooding is expected. Treat it as real and act on it. Do not wait to see the water yourself, because the water can rise faster than people expect.

## F2. Prepare a plan and a grab bag

Agree in advance on a meeting point on higher ground, a primary route and a backup route that both stay above likely water. Make sure everyone in your group knows them.

Keep a grab bag that can be picked up in seconds:
- drinking water and long-life food for one day
- a torch with spare batteries and a charged power bank
- a basic first aid kit and any regular medicines
- copies of important documents in a waterproof bag
- warm clothes and a whistle

Do not plan to take heavy belongings. Documents and medicines matter more than furniture or equipment.

## F3. When a flood warning is issued

1. Listen to the official instructions. If you are told to evacuate, go.
2. Gather the group and the grab bag.
3. Check first on people who need help to move: people with limited mobility, young children, older people.
4. Move to higher ground by the safe route, before the water arrives.

Moving property to an upper floor is only for time that is left over after people are safe.

## F4. Moving to higher ground

Choose a route that stays above the water, even if it is longer. A longer dry route is safer than a short wet one.

Keep the group together and count heads at the start and at the end. Tell someone outside the group where you are going.

## F5. The dangers of floodwater

- **Depth and current.** Moving water about 15 cm deep can knock an adult off their feet. About 30 cm can float a car. You cannot judge depth or current by looking.
- **Hidden hazards.** Open drains, holes, debris and damaged surfaces are covered by the water.
- **Contamination.** Floodwater can carry sewage, chemicals and bacteria, even when it looks clear. Avoid contact and wash well after any contact.
- **Electricity.** Water and electrical equipment are a deadly mix. Stay away from fallen cables and do not touch wet electrical equipment.

Never walk, wade or drive through floodwater to save time. Never send one person ahead to test the depth.

## F6. Getting help and helping others

Call 112 and give the location, the number of people, and anyone who needs urgent help. When others are present, point at one named person: "You, call 112, then tell me what they said." A general request leads people to assume someone else has called.

Do not enter fast-moving water to rescue someone. Call for help, and from a safe position, reach with a long object or throw something that floats.

## F7. After the flood

Do not return until the authorities say the area is safe. Water may still be high in places, and structures may be weakened.

- Treat tap water as unsafe for drinking until it is officially announced as safe.
- Do not switch on electrical equipment or lights in a wet building. Have the electrics checked first.
- Photograph damage before cleaning, for records and claims.
- Do not pump out a flooded basement quickly. Remove the water gradually, because rapid pumping can damage walls and floors.
- Clean and disinfect surfaces that touched floodwater, and wear gloves and boots.
